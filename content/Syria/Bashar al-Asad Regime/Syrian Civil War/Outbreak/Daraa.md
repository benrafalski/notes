
# Overview
- “Cradle of the Syrian Revolution”
	- first major protests in 2011
	- began after local teenagers were arrested for writing anti-government graffiti
	- government’s harsh response ignited nationwide unrest.
- Strategic Importance:
	- Sits near border of Israel + Jordan
	- supply routes, refugee flows, foreign influence
- Armed opposition
	- became one of first regions where armed opposition groups gained influence
	- For several years, much of the area was controlled by rebels
	- rebel groups surrendered or accepted reconciliation deals in 2018 after Russian intervention
	- Groups:
		- Southern Front (FSA)
		- Ahrar al-Sham (Islamist)
		- Khalid ibn al-Walid Army (ISIS)

# Outbreak
- Why protests started specifically in Daraa
	- Arab Spring starts
	- group of teenagers wrote anti-government graffiti on a school wall with slogans like “The people want the fall of the regime.”
	- Local security forces arrested the teenagers and allegedly tortured them
	- When their families demanded their release, local authorities reportedly mocked or dismissed them
	- led to public protests demanding the boys’ release and government reforms.
	- Security forces responded with force, only causing more protests
- Tribal structure
	- Strong tribal and family networks
	- Rural agricultural communities
	- Close social ties across towns and villages
	- When the teenagers were arrested, their families and tribes mobilized quickly
- Early Uprising
	- First protests demanded: release of prisoners, reforms, an end to corruption and police abuses
	- government’s crackdown escalated tensions: Security forces fired on demonstrators, army was deployed to the city, neighborhoods were surrounded or shelled
- Strategic importance:
	- Jordan border: refugees, rebel supply and smuggling routes, foreign support
	- Southern Front rebels
- Long term:
	- 2018 Russia + Government offensive: Many rebel groups accepted negotiated surrender deals, and Daraa returned to formal government control.

### 2018 Reconciliation
- Rebels surrender heavy weapons
	- Fighters can choose: reconciliation with the government or move to Idlib
	- Most reconciled, some stayed and became “reconciled fighters.”
- Continued Violence post 2018
	- many former rebels kept light weapons
	- local militias remained active
	- assassinations became common

# Rebel Southern Supply Line
- Overview:
	- Weapons, money, and coordination flowed from Jordan into southern Syria
	- This pipeline was controlled and tightly managed by foreign governments
	- The goal was to support “moderate” rebel groups, not jihadists
		- Was in contrast to the more independent and less centralized supply line in northern Syria from Turkey
- MOC (Military Operations Center)
	- Located in Amman
	- Highly Controlled:
		- which rebel groups got weapons
		- how much funding they received
		- what operations they were allowed to carry out
	- Foreign governments: US (leader), Jordan, Saudi Arabia, UAE, UK/France (to lesser extent)
	- Funding:
		- rebel salaries
		- light arms, some anti-tank missiles
		- intelligence and planning support
	- Restrictions
		- discouraged attacks on Israel
		- avoided escalation that could trigger full regional war
		- prevent jihadist groups from gaining strength
	- Ultimate Failure:
	- US ended support
		- [December 2017](https://production-tcf.imgix.net/app/uploads/2017/10/03111222/saving-americas-syrian-ceasefire.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com): US ends Timber Sycamore in July 2017
	- Russia air support which they couldn't defend
		- [June 2018](https://www.reuters.com/article/world/syrian-rebels-say-us-wont-intervene-in-south-syria-idUSKBN1JK00T/): US says don't expect help during Russia intervention
- Rebels Funded:
	- Southern Front (FSA Aligned)
		- First Army
		- Yarmouk Army
		- Southern Division (US-backed)
		- Omari Brigades
- Rebels Avoided
	- Ahrar al-Sham (sometimes tolerated, but not central)
	- Khalid ibn al-Walid Army
		- Often fought with Southern Front
