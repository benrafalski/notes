# Coal
## Federal Coal Use
- On September 29, the Trump admin said it would open [13.1 million acres of federal land for coal leasing](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/climate-energy/trump-officials-announce-plan-boost-coal-output-2025-09-29/). Coal plants generated 15% of U.S. electricity in 2024, a fall from 50% in 2000, with the coal workforce sinking to 40,000 from 70,000 in 2015.
- February 11, 2026: Trump signs EO <a href="https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2026-03156" target="_blank">14386</a> ordering the Defense Department to [purchase electricity from coal-fired power plants](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/trump-directs-energy-department-issue-funds-keep-coal-plants-online-2026-02-11/). The president also announced that the Energy Department will provide six coal plants in Kentucky, North Carolina, Ohio, Virginia and West Virginia with $175 million for upgrades.
- June 4: Trump used Defense Production Act to to fund [$425 million in upgrades to 13 coal-fired power plants](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/trump-unveil-700-million-coal-support-plan-using-emergency-powers-2026-06-04/) and $75 million to support the proposed West Gateway coal export terminal in Oakland, California. The Energy Department also said it was finalizing up to $350 million in previously announced funding to help develop four coal facility projects, including new power plants in Alaska and West Virginia.
- **Michigan Coal Plant** (September 11): unanimous ruling by a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit found the [Department of Energy exceeded its authority](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/us-court-blocks-trump-administration-bid-keep-michigan-coal-plant-open-2026-09-11/) by using emergency powers under the Federal Power Act. The DOE instructed Michigan's 1960s-era J.H. Campbell plant in 2025 ​to remain online shortly before its planned closure. The plant had been slated to close in 2025 because it was no longer ​economic. Its continued operation under the order cost the utility $295 million from May 2025 ​through June 2026.
## Deregulation
- February 20: EPA announces [roll back of air regulations for coal power plants](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/trump-epa-weaken-rule-limiting-harmful-mercury-air-toxics-coal-plants-2026-02-20/) limiting mercury and hazardous air toxics. They cited the alleviation of costs for utilities that run older coal plants at a time when demand for power is soaring amid the expansion of data centers used for artificial intelligence. But environmental groups said weakening standards for mercury, a neurotoxin that can impair babies' brain development, and other air toxics will lead to higher health-related costs. The Biden-era Mercury and Air Toxics Standard, which updated standards set in 2012 by Obama, would reduce allowable mercury pollution from the coal plants by 70%, emissions of nickel, arsenic, lead and other toxic metals from coal plants by two-thirds and result in health cost savings of $420 million through 2037.  The EPA said the 2012 MATS rule provides "an ample margin of safety to protect public health," and that its proposed 2024 additions would cost more than they benefit. Coal-burning power plants are among the largest sources of hazardous air pollution, including mercury, lead, arsenic, and acid gases, as well as major sources of benzene, formaldehyde, dioxins and other organic hazardous air pollutants.
## Emissions Impact
- June 30: Report from Ember, Kearney Institute and KPMG finds global energy-related carbon emissions from the production and use of energy, rose 1.1% in 2025 with about [13.3% of that increase came from the United States](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/us-leads-global-co2-emissions-increase-2025-report-finds-2026-06-29/). The jump was due to U.S. coal consumption jumping 10% in 2025.
