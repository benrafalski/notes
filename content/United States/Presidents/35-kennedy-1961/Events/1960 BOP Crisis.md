==The U.S. balance of payments crisis during the Kennedy administration stemmed from an ongoing deficit where more U.S. dollars were leaving the country (via foreign aid, military spending, and private investment) than coming in==. Under the Bretton Woods System, foreign governments could convert those surplus dollars into gold at a fixed rate of $35 per ounce, which caused a massive drain on the U.S. gold gold reserves and threatened the stability of the dollar.

---

## 🔎 What the Kennedy Administration Did

President Kennedy viewed the gold drain as a top national security threat and deployed a multi-front strategy to defend the dollar without resorting to isolationist trade barriers:

- Operation Twist (1961): The [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/) and Treasury intentionally raised short-term interest rates to keep foreign capital from fleeing the U.S., while simultaneously lowering long-term interest rates to stimulate domestic economic growth.
- The London Gold Pool (1961): The U.S. partnered with seven European central banks to collaboratively sell gold on the open market if the price rose above $35 an ounce, deterring private speculation.
- Capital Controls & Tax Disincentives: The administration introduced the Interest Equalization Tax (IET) on foreign securities to discourage Americans from investing in overseas assets, keeping capital at home.
- Military and Trade Offsets: Kennedy pressured NATO allies, particularly West Germany, to buy American military equipment to offset the cost of maintaining U.S. troops on foreign soil.

---

## 📊 Was the Issue Solved?

The crisis was temporarily managed but not fundamentally solved.

While Kennedy's targeted interventions successfully prevented an immediate run on the U.S. gold vault and stabilized the market through his presidency, they only treated the symptoms of a deeper structural flaw. The underlying deficit persisted because the global economy required more dollars for liquidity than the U.S. gold stock could back.

Ultimately, these unresolved pressures escalated through the late 1960s, forcing President Richard Nixon to permanently end the dollar's convertibility into gold in 1971, effectively dismantling the Bretton Woods system.
